Week 3 · Lesson 01 · AI for Doctors

The AI-augmented clinician.

AI will not replace clinicians. But a clinician who builds durable workflows around AI will spend less of the day fighting the keyboard and more of it with patients. This lesson is not about a tool or a prompt. It is about a working relationship — where AI drafts and surfaces at speed, and the clinician verifies, decides, and remains the one accountable to the patient. One rule holds it together: AI suggests; the clinician verifies and decides.

A note before we begin. This is professional education for clinicians about clinical workflows — it is not medical advice, and it does not tell you how to treat any patient. Nothing here recommends a specific tool. Many AI tools are not cleared or approved medical devices, and none should ever receive identifiable patient data through consumer channels.
01 / 04

AI suggests, the clinician verifies and decides

Before any tool, learn the one rule that keeps the rest of this week honest: AI is a fast, tireless drafting and pattern-surfacing partner. It is not a colleague, not an authority on medicine, and it is never the diagnostician or decision-maker of record. That last phrase carries the weight of the profession — accountability runs to the patient, and it cannot be delegated to a machine.

Think of general-purpose AI the way an attending thinks of a very well-read student who has memorised a library but has never examined this patient, cannot be licensed, and has never been responsible for an outcome. Ask for a plain-language draft of after-visit instructions and you get one in seconds. Ask it to reword a dense note, to list questions a worried patient might ask, or to summarise a long message thread, and it produces all of them. This is genuinely useful: the documentation and the inbox are the most draining, least human parts of the clinical day, and AI compresses both. But that student has not laid hands on the patient, cannot see what is not in the text in front of it, will invent a reference or a lab value that sounds perfect and does not exist, and states a wrong clinical claim with the same fluent confidence it uses for a right one.

So the workflow is always two-handed. One hand suggests — drafts, summaries, first passes, rewordings, differentials to consider. The other hand verifies and decides — what the patient's story and exam actually show, what the evidence really says, what is safe and appropriate for this person, and what enters the record under the clinician's name. The suggesting hand is fast and cheap. The verifying-and-deciding hand is slow, examined, licensed, and the thing the patient is actually relying on. Every stage of clinical work runs on this split. Why the line never moves — and why Decide is different

Notice that across all five stages, the verifying-and-deciding hand keeps the same jobs: confirming what is actually true of this patient, judging what the evidence supports, owning the plan, and standing behind every word that enters the record. But look at stage three. In Gather, Reason, Document and Communicate, AI may draft and suggest. In Decide, its column is nearly empty — because the diagnosis and the plan are the practice of medicine itself. AI can lay out options to consider; it must never be the diagnostician or decision-maker of record. A clinician who guards that stage can adopt any tool without fear; one who surrenders it has stopped practising medicine.

02 / 04

A clinician's day, reclaimed

The promise of AI in clinical work is not "less medicine." It is less of the low-value, keyboard-bound labour that surrounds medicine — the documentation and inbox burden widely blamed for burnout — so that more of the day can go to the part patients actually come for: attention, examination, reasoning and human presence.

Most clinicians lose hours every day to work that is necessary but not the point: typing up the visit note, reformatting a referral, drafting the routine message reply, restructuring a long handoff, retyping the same patient-education paragraph for the twentieth time. The documentation and message inbox in particular have become a defining source of clinician burnout — hours of after-hours "pyjama time" spent facing a screen instead of a patient. AI is at its best exactly here: on the high-volume, template-shaped, first-pass tasks that drain a day without adding a single element of clinical judgment.

Toggle the switch below to compare an ordinary day before and with AI. Watch the grey admin blocks and the tan documentation grind shrink — and watch the blue "clinical presence" time, the examination, reasoning and conversation only a clinician can provide, expand into the space that opens up. The hours do not vanish; they move from the keyboard toward the patient, and toward a day that ends at a sane hour. A caution named up front: every AI-drafted note and message must still be read and corrected by the clinician before it is signed — the time saved is real, but it is never unsupervised.

Reclaimed, not erased

The honest version of this figure: AI does not hand a clinician a shorter day for free. It gives a better-spent one. The minutes it saves on note-writing and routine replies are minutes you reinvest in listening, examining and reasoning — or in going home before dark. But an AI scribe that mishears, a summary that quietly drops a critical detail, or a drafted reply sent without a careful read can do real harm. Reclaimed time only helps the patient if every draft still passes under the clinician's eyes before it becomes part of care.

03 / 04

Should AI touch this task?

Not every task is a safe place for AI. Before you delegate anything, run it through the gate below — but note first that two dials are not dials at all. They are hard vetoes. Patient privacy is one: identifiable patient information must never enter a consumer AI tool. Autonomous clinical decision-making is the other: AI must never be the diagnosis or the decision of record. Either veto, on its own, ends the conversation.

The privacy veto comes first, on purpose. Pasting an identifiable patient's history, images or results into a general, non-approved consumer tool can breach HIPAA, GDPR and equivalent duties, and expose that data to storage, training or disclosure. No efficiency gain survives that. The second veto is just as hard: if the task is the diagnosis, the prescription, or the disposition — the decision of record — AI cannot make it, however capable it seems. Only once both vetoes are clear do the softer dials matter. Safety / stakes: how much rides on getting this exactly right — a plain-language reword is low, a discharge plan is high. Verifiability: how easily you can check the output yourself — you can confirm a rewritten instruction in seconds; you cannot casually trust a cited dose or reference without checking the source. And note the fourth input: regulatory status — many tools are not cleared or approved medical devices, and using an uncleared tool for a task that looks like a medical-device function is its own risk.

Set the two vetoes and move the dials to describe a real task, then read the verdict. The pattern to internalise: AI is safest on de-identified, low-stakes, high-verifiability, non-decisional work — and categorically off-limits the moment identifiable patient data would touch a consumer tool, or the moment the task becomes the decision itself. When in doubt, the machine drafts on de-identified or general material and you verify; it never decides, and it never sees data it shouldn't.

The rule under the dials

High stakes plus low verifiability is the danger zone for hallucinated findings, invented references and wrong doses — and privacy and autonomous decision-making are the two trapdoors beneath the whole floor. The safe move is never a flat "don't use AI." It is: clear both vetoes first, remember that many tools are not cleared medical devices, then let AI draft the structure on de-identified or general material — and put your own verified, examined judgment on top before anything reaches the patient or the record.

04 / 04

Four myths, flipped

Every conversation about AI in medicine carries some version of these four beliefs — two from the enthusiasts, two from the sceptics. Flip each card to see the more honest version. Neither breathless hype nor flat refusal survives contact with a real patient, a real chart, or a real outcome.

The goal of this lesson is not to make you enthusiastic or fearful, but accurate. AI in clinical work is a powerful drafting and surfacing tool with real, named limitations — patient-privacy risk, hallucinated references and findings, embedded bias and health-inequity, and the fact that many tools are not cleared medical devices — that you manage the way you manage any clinical risk: by knowing exactly where it lives and refusing to look away from it.

More honestly

AI hallucinates — it invents references, guideline numbers, doses and lab values in the same confident voice it uses for real ones. Fluency is not evidence. Every clinical claim, citation and dose must be checked against a primary, current source before it touches a patient or a note. A confident sentence is not a verified one.

More honestly

AI learned from historical medical data that already encodes inequity — in who was studied, who was believed, whose pain was recorded. Used unchecked, it can quietly widen gaps in care across race, sex and background. Health equity is a judgment only the clinician supplies; the model will not flag the bias it inherited.

More honestly

Refusing a drafting tool to spend evenings retyping notes is not rigour — it is documentation burden that pulls attention away from patients. The one-sentence version

The AI-augmented clinician lets the machine draft and surface at speed on de-identified, checkable, lower-stakes work — and keeps every diagnosis, every plan, every verified fact, and every duty to the patient firmly in human hands.

What to carry forward

You now have the working relationship at the heart of this week. AI is a fast drafting and surfacing partner with real limits; you are the accountable clinician. Clear the privacy and no-autonomous-decision vetoes first, run every task through the delegation gate, verify every finding and reference before it reaches a patient or the record, and never let a confident sentence stand in for a checked one. The next lesson puts this relationship to work on the first real task: finding, appraising and applying clinical evidence with AI.
