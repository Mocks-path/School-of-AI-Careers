Week 3 · Lesson 02 · AI for Doctors

Clinical evidence, verified.

AI can find an answer to almost any clinical question in seconds. That is exactly the danger. A confident summary, a clean-looking citation, a precise-sounding dose — all can be fabricated, out of date, or simply wrong for the patient in front of you. This lesson builds the durable skill that makes AI safe for evidence work: a workflow with a verification gate in the middle that nothing gets past unchecked.

A note before we begin. This is professional education for clinicians about evidence workflows — it is not medical advice, and no example here is a treatment recommendation. AI is decision-support only; the clinician appraises the evidence and decides. Never enter identifiable patient data into a consumer AI tool, and remember that many tools are not cleared or approved medical devices.
01 / 03

The evidence workflow, with a gate in the middle

Answering a clinical question well has always followed a shape: ask a focused question, search for evidence, appraise it, synthesise, and apply it to this patient. AI can accelerate every step — but it also introduces a new failure mode at the search step, where a fabricated answer looks identical to a real one. So the workflow gains a hard gate: APPRAISE & VERIFY, which nothing passes unchecked.

Older evidence teaching moved smoothly from search to synthesis, because a search returned real sources you could open. AI changes that. When you ask a general model a clinical question, it does not retrieve a source — it generates the most plausible-sounding text, which may include references, doses and figures that do not exist. The output arrives polished and confident whether or not it is true. That is why the verification step is no longer a nicety at the end; it is a gate in the middle of the workflow that every AI-generated claim must pass before it is allowed to become part of your reasoning.

Step through the flow below. Notice how stages one, two, four and five are ordinary evidence work — but the third, APPRAISE & VERIFY, is drawn differently on purpose. It is the point where you stop trusting fluency and start checking primary sources: does the reference exist, is it the current guideline, is the dose right, and does it actually apply to this patient. AI is decision-support up to that gate and after it; the appraisal itself is yours.

Why the gate faces both ways

The verification gate does more than catch fabrications. It also faces forward, toward the patient: even a real, correctly cited, current guideline may not apply to the person in front of you — wrong age, wrong comorbidity, wrong context, a contraindication the study excluded. So the gate asks two questions at once: is this evidence true, and is it true for this patient. AI can help you reach the gate faster and can even help you read what you find. It cannot walk through the gate for you. That step is clinical judgment, and it is the reason the profession, not the tool, remains accountable.

Ungrounded vs grounded: the same question, two answers

The single most important habit in this lesson is learning to distrust a fluent answer. An ungrounded model will hand you a confident citation and a precise dose that were never checked against a real source — and it will look exactly as trustworthy as a verified one. Toggle between the two answers below and watch the verification checklist decide which you can actually use.

Both answers below respond to the same clinical question. The ungrounded one is what a general model may produce when asked directly: a real-sounding reference, a specific figure, a confident tone — none of it traced to a primary source. The grounded one is the same information after it has been through the gate: checked against a current, primary reference, with the dose confirmed and the fit to this patient considered. The words can look almost identical. The difference is entirely in whether each claim survived verification — which is why the checklist, not the fluency, is what you trust.

The four-line verification checklist

Carry this checklist to every AI-generated clinical claim, and refuse to use anything that fails a line. Exists: open the actual reference — a plausible title, author and year mean nothing until the source is real. Current guideline: confirm it is the up-to-date recommendation, not a superseded one the model absorbed years ago. Correct dose / figure: check every number against the primary source, because a single wrong digit is a patient-safety event. Applies to this patient: even true, current evidence may not fit this person's age, comorbidities or context. A claim that clears all four has earned a place in your reasoning; one that fails any is discarded, no matter how confident it sounded.

Sharpen the question: the PICO builder

A vague question gets a vague, hard-to-verify answer — the exact conditions in which AI fills the gaps with plausible invention. The oldest tool in evidence-based practice fixes this: PICO. Naming the Patient, the Intervention, the Comparison and the Outcome turns a woolly query into a precise, answerable, checkable one. Add each element below and watch a vague question sharpen into one you can actually verify.

PICO is not about the AI — it is about you thinking clearly before you ask. "What's the best treatment?" is unanswerable and invites a confident guess. But name the Patient (who, specifically), the Intervention (what you are considering), the Comparison (against what alternative), and the Outcome (that actually matters to this patient), and the question becomes narrow enough that a real, verifiable answer exists — and narrow enough that you can tell whether the AI's answer is on target or drifting. A sharp question is the first line of defence against a fluent wrong one.

Why the sharp question is also the safe question

A well-built PICO question does two jobs at once. It gets you a better answer — narrow enough that a real body of evidence addresses it directly. And it gets you a safer answer, because a specific question is one you can actually verify: you know what to search for, what the numbers should look like, and whether the reply fits the patient you named. Vagueness is where hallucination hides. Precision is where verification becomes possible. The sharp question and the safe workflow are, in the end, the same discipline.

What to carry forward

Evidence work with AI is fast, and its speed is exactly why it needs a gate. Ask a sharp PICO question, let AI help you search and read — but treat every generated citation, dose and figure as unverified until you have opened the primary source, confirmed it is current, checked the number, and judged whether it fits this patient. AI is decision-support up to and after that gate. The appraisal, and the decision, are yours.

  • The workflow has a hard gate: Ask → Search → APPRAISE & VERIFY → Synthesise → Apply
  • A fluent, confident answer can be fully fabricated — distrust fluency, trust the checklist
  • Verify four things: exists · current guideline · correct dose · applies to this patient
  • A sharp PICO question is also the safe question — vagueness is where hallucination hides
  • AI is decision-support; the appraisal and the clinical decision stay with the clinician
