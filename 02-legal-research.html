Week 2 · Lesson 02 · AI for Lawyers

Legal research, verified.

AI can compress a day of legal research into minutes — and it can invent an authority that looks perfect and does not exist, in the same breath. This lesson is about the durable workflow that keeps the speed and removes the danger: frame the question well, let AI generate leads, and pass everything through a non-negotiable verification gate before a single citation reaches a brief or a client.

01 / 03

The research workflow — and its one gate

Research with AI is not "ask and paste." It is a chain of six moves, and one of them is a gate that everything must pass through. Skip the gate and you have not saved time — you have manufactured risk at speed. Learn the whole flow, then learn to guard the gate.

Traditional research is slow but self-correcting: you read the actual case, so you know it exists and what it holds. AI research is fast but not self-correcting: the model produces fluent text that may describe a real authority, a distorted one, or a wholly invented one, and it cannot tell you which. The workflow below keeps the speed of generation while restoring the discipline of verification. The critical move is stage four — VERIFY — where every proposition and every citation the model produced is pulled to primary source, confirmed to exist, and confirmed to still be good law. Nothing crosses that gate unchecked.

Click through the stages. Notice that stages one to three are cheap and forgiving — a bad frame just wastes a prompt. From the VERIFY gate onward, mistakes become expensive: a false citation in a synthesis becomes a false citation in a filing. That is why the gate is drawn in a different colour, and why it never gets skipped.

The gate is the profession

Everything before VERIFY is a machine being fast. Everything from VERIFY onward is a lawyer being accountable. A model that hands you ten perfect-looking authorities has done nothing useful until you have confirmed which of them are real, on point, and still good law. The verification gate is not a step you add when you have time — it is the step that turns AI output into legal work you can sign.

02 / 03

Ungrounded vs grounded

The single most dangerous thing AI does in legal research is state a false authority with total confidence. The defence is not to distrust everything — it is to ground every claim in a primary source you have opened yourself. Toggle between the two and watch the same fluent paragraph go from liability to asset.

An ungrounded answer is generated from the model's patterns alone. It reads beautifully, cites a case with a plausible name and a real-looking citation, and quotes a holding that fits your argument perfectly — because it was written to fit, not because it was found. Courts across jurisdictions have sanctioned lawyers who filed exactly this. A grounded answer is the same paragraph after verification: every citation pulled to the actual reporter, every quote checked against the real text, every case confirmed as still good law. The prose barely changes. The professional risk changes completely.

Verification checklist

    Fluency is not authority

    Internalise the tell: the more perfectly a citation fits your argument, the more you owe it a check. Real law is messy and rarely lines up flawlessly; a suspiciously clean holding is a reason for suspicion, not relief. The verification checklist is short and always the same — does the case exist, does the quote match, is it the right court, is it still good law, does it actually say what the model claims. Run it every time, on every citation, before anything is filed.

    03 / 03

    The research brief builder

    A vague question gets a vague, ungroundable answer — and vague answers are exactly where hallucinations hide. The fastest way to make AI research safer is to sharpen the question before you ask it. Add each element below and watch a hopeless query become a researchable one.

    "What's the law on non-competes?" is not a research question; it is a conversation opener that invites the model to guess across every jurisdiction and era at once. A researchable question is specific about four things: the jurisdiction whose law binds you, the area of law, the concrete facts that make your matter yours, and the procedural posture that shapes what you actually need. Each element you add does two jobs: it narrows what the model can plausibly invent, and it gives you a sharper standard to verify the answer against. Toggle the chips and watch the query — and the sharpness meter — improve.

    The question is half the verification

    A sharp question does not just get a better answer — it makes the answer checkable. When you have named the jurisdiction, the area, the facts and the posture, you know exactly what a correct authority must satisfy, and a wrong or invented one has nowhere to hide. Vague in, ungroundable out. The discipline of framing is the same discipline as verifying, applied a step earlier.

    What to carry forward

    Legal research with AI keeps its speed only when it keeps its gate. Frame the question with jurisdiction, area, facts and posture; let AI generate leads and first-pass synthesis; then pass every proposition and every citation through verification to primary source before it reaches a synthesis, a brief, or a client. Fluency is never authority, and a perfect-sounding citation is a reason to check, not to trust. The next lesson takes this verified research into the drafting of documents that actually leave your desk.
