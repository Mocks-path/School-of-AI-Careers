Week 1 · Lesson 02 · AI for Teachers

Planning with intent.

A vague request to AI produces a vague lesson. The teachers who get the most from these tools aren't better at "prompting" — they're clearer about what they're trying to achieve. This lesson pairs an old, durable planning discipline with a new, fast drafting partner: you name the destination, AI drafts the road, and you keep the wheel.

01 / 04

Start from the end

Backward design is decades older than AI, and it is the single habit that makes AI planning work. Instead of starting with a fun activity, you start with what students should be able to do — and only then design backward to get them there.

The failure mode AI makes worse is "activity-first" planning: you ask for a fun lesson on volcanoes, get a lively worksheet, and only later notice it never actually taught the standard. AI is very good at generating engaging activities and completely indifferent to whether they teach anything. Backward design closes that gap by forcing the order: objective first, evidence second, activities last.

The five stages below are the durable workflow. You define the objective and the success criteria — the destination and how you'll know you've arrived. AI then drafts activities, differentiation, and checks against that fixed target. Because the target is yours and explicit, everything AI produces can be judged against it. Why the order is non-negotiable

If you let AI pick activities before you've fixed the objective, you'll get motion without direction — busy students learning something, but not necessarily the thing you're accountable for. Naming the objective and success criteria first turns AI from a generator of nice-looking filler into a genuine design partner, because now every draft it hands you has a standard to be measured against.

02 / 04

The brief builder

"Write a lesson on fractions" and "Design a 40-minute lesson for a mixed-ability Grade 4 class introducing equivalent fractions, aligned to our standard, with a quick exit check" produce wildly different results. The gap between them is not magic. It is four pieces of context you already carry in your head.

A good brief is not longer for its own sake — it's specified. Adding the audience, the grade, the standard, and the time removes the four things AI would otherwise guess at, usually wrongly. Each detail you add is a decision you're keeping in human hands instead of surrendering to the model's defaults.

Add the chips below one at a time and watch a vague request sharpen into a usable brief — and the output on the right sharpen with it. This is the whole skill: you're not learning secret phrasing, you're just refusing to make AI guess what you already know.

The teacher still decides

A tight brief gets you a strong first draft — not a finished lesson. Even at 100% specificity, AI doesn't know that two of your students read three years below grade level, that the projector is broken this week, or that this class needs movement after lunch. The brief removes the wrong guesses; your judgment supplies the right details AI could never know.

03 / 04

One activity, three tiers

Differentiation is where AI earns its keep and where it most needs supervision. Producing a support version, a core version, and an extension of the same task used to be an hour of quiet duplication. AI drafts all three in a minute — but you still decide which child lands in which tier, and that decision is the pedagogy.

The move is simple: fix the activity and the objective, then ask AI to draft three access points to the same learning. Support lowers the barrier without lowering the goal — scaffolds, sentence starters, worked examples. Core is the grade-level task as designed. Extension deepens rather than merely adding more — asking students to justify, compare, or apply. Crucially, all three aim at the same objective; you are varying the road, not the destination.

Same destination, different roads

The trap AI can lead you into is "more work for the fast, less learning for the slow" — handing strong students extra questions and weak students a watered-down goal. Real differentiation keeps the objective constant. Use AI to draft the scaffolds and the stretch, then apply the judgment it can't: which student, which tier, and when to quietly move them up.

Four planning disciplines

Before you close this lesson, four habits separate teachers who plan with AI from those who are merely planned for by it. Flip each card for the discipline underneath the temptation.

None of these say "plan less." They say: keep the decisions that make a lesson yours, and let AI carry the volume. A plan drafted in one minute and judged for ten beats a plan typed from scratch in an hour — but only if the ten minutes of judgment actually happen.

The discipline

Plan with roles, not identities. "A student reading below grade level" is safe; a real name and diagnosis in a general tool is not. Strip the private details — the plan works just as well without them.

The one-sentence version

Good AI planning is backward design with a fast intern: you fix the objective and success criteria, brief AI with real context, and judge every draft — including its differentiation — against the destination you set.

What to carry forward

You now have a durable planning workflow that survives any tool. Start from the objective, brief AI with audience, grade, standard and time, and let it draft activities and tiers you then judge against your success criteria. The teacher sets the destination and checks the fit; AI drafts the road. Next week, we take this discipline into the classroom itself — teaching and assessing with AI alongside you.

  • Backward design first: objective and success criteria before activities
  • A good brief adds audience, grade, standard and time — no guessing
  • Differentiate the road, not the destination — one objective, three access points
  • Judge every draft against your success criteria, not its polish
  • Plan with roles, not real student identities
