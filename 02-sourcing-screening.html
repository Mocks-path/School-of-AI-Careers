Week 4 · Lesson 02 · AI for Recruiters

Sourcing & screening, done fairly.

Sourcing and screening are where a recruiter's week disappears — and where AI's help is most tempting and most dangerous. Done well, AI widens your net, drafts warmer outreach, and organises a mountain of applications so you can spend your time on people. Done carelessly, it quietly filters candidates out on proxies for race, gender and age, and creates adverse impact no one intended. This lesson is about building a sourcing-and-screening workflow where AI is genuine support — and a human always holds the gate before anyone is shortlisted or ruled out.

A note before we begin. This is professional education for recruiters about fair sourcing and screening — it is jurisdiction-neutral and is not legal advice. Nothing here recommends a specific tool. AI here is decision-support only: it never rejects a candidate on its own, and identifiable candidate personal data must never enter a consumer AI tool.
01 / 03

The workflow, with a human at the gate

Sourcing and screening is not one act but a chain: define what the role really needs, write an inclusive job description, source widely, screen as support, and shortlist — with a human reviewing before anyone is advanced or ruled out. AI can lend a hand at every step. But one step is special: the human-review gate is not optional and cannot be handed to a machine.

The temptation with a large applicant pile is to let AI "just handle the filtering" — to hand it the pile and take back a shortlist. That is exactly the move that turns a helpful tool into a discrimination engine. The moment AI closes a door on a candidate without a human looking, you have an automated adverse decision: the place where inherited bias, proxy inference and hallucinated qualifications do their quiet damage, and where legal exposure concentrates. So we design the workflow so that AI does the heavy, tireless first-pass work — summarising, organising, drafting, matching against job-related criteria — and a human always holds the final gate before the shortlist is set or anyone is rejected.

The gate is the whole point

Steps one through four can all be AI-supported and still be safe, because none of them close a door on their own — they draft, widen and organise. Step five is where a person's application lives or dies, and it is deliberately built as a human-review gate: a recruiter reads the AI's first-pass work, checks it against job-related criteria, watches for proxy bias, and owns the shortlist. AI never advances or rejects a candidate by itself. Build the chain this way and you get the speed without surrendering the fairness or the accountability.

02 / 03

The inclusive job description

Screening fairly starts long before the applications arrive — it starts with the words in the job ad. Language quietly signals who is welcome to apply. Gendered, ageist and exclusionary phrasing shrinks and skews your pool before AI ever touches it. AI can draft a job description in seconds; the recruiter's job is to catch and rewrite the language that turns people away.

An AI drafting tool is genuinely useful here — it can produce a first pass fast, and it can suggest more inclusive phrasing when you ask. But it can just as easily reproduce the tired, exclusionary language it learned from a million old job ads. So the skill is not "let AI write the JD." It is: let AI draft, then read it as an inclusion editor. Toggle between the two versions below. The red highlights mark language that narrows or skews the pool — gendered words, coded "culture" signals, ageist markers, and needless barriers. The green shows the rewritten, inclusive version: the same role, described in words that invite a wider, fairer pool to apply.

Words are a screen too

Every exclusionary phrase in a job ad is a silent screen that filters people out before you ever see them — and unlike a rubric, it leaves no trace. "Young and energetic" tells older candidates not to bother. "He'll thrive" tells women it may not be for them. "Native English speaker" screens out fluent non-native speakers with no job-related reason. Let AI help you draft and even suggest inclusive alternatives, but the final read — checking every word for who it quietly excludes — is a human judgment you own.

03 / 03

Rubric vs proxy bias

When AI helps screen, everything depends on what it is asked to weigh. A screen built on job-related criteria — the skills, experience and competencies the role actually requires — supports a fair decision. A screen that lets AI infer from names, schools, postcodes or employment gaps is a proxy-bias machine that can create adverse impact, whatever the intent. Toggle between the two below to see the difference.

The safe pattern is a transparent, job-related rubric: define the criteria that genuinely predict success in the role, and have AI score or organise candidates against those — as support, for a human to review. The dangerous pattern is letting AI weigh things that are not job-related but correlate with protected characteristics: a "prestigious" school, a "native-sounding" name, a career gap, a home neighbourhood. These are proxies. Even with no discriminatory intent, screening on them can produce adverse impact — a selection rate for one group that falls below the widely-cited four-fifths (80%) benchmark of another's, a classic warning sign that a process may be discriminatory and needs review. AI will never flag this on its own; you have to design it out and check for it.

Support, never sentence

Even a clean, job-related rubric is decision-support, not the decision. AI can score candidates against your criteria and organise them for review, but it never sets the shortlist alone and never auto-rejects. And the moment a screen weighs anything that stands in for race, gender, age, or background, you are one careless step from adverse impact. Keep the criteria job-related, keep a human at the gate, and check your selection rates across groups — the four-fifths benchmark is a smoke alarm, not a target to game.

What to carry forward

Sourcing and screening is where AI saves the most time and where it can do the most quiet harm. Build the chain so AI supports every step — defining criteria, drafting an inclusive JD, sourcing widely, screening as first-pass work — and a human always holds the gate before anyone is shortlisted or rejected. Read every AI-drafted job ad as an inclusion editor, keep every screen on job-related criteria, and never let AI infer from names, schools or gaps. AI is decision-support; you are the fair, accountable recruiter. The next lesson takes this into the interview and offer stage.

  • The human-review gate is non-negotiable — AI never advances or rejects alone
  • Job ads are a silent screen — rewrite gendered, ageist, exclusionary language
  • Score on job-related criteria; never let AI infer from names, schools or gaps
  • Watch for adverse impact — the four-fifths (80%) rule is a warning sign to review
  • No candidate PII in consumer tools; AI is decision-support, never the decision
