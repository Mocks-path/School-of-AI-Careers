Week 1 · AI for Teachers · Lesson 03

Assessment that stays human.

The point of assessment isn't to produce a number — it's to understand where a learner is and help them move. AI can draft rubrics, sift first-pass comments, and catch patterns across a class in minutes. But the judgement of what a piece of work is worth, and what a child needs to hear, is a professional act that stays with you. This lesson is about the workflow, not the tool.

01 / 04

The assessment loop — and where AI belongs in it

Good assessment is a cycle, not a moment. Every stage has a mechanical half AI can accelerate and a judgement half only a teacher can hold. Knowing which is which is the whole skill.

Beginners reach for AI at the wrong end of the loop — they ask it to award grades. Professionals put AI where the work is repetitive and reversible: drafting items, structuring rubrics, generating first-pass comments a human then edits. It has never met your class, doesn't know that one student's grandmother died last week, and can't see the flicker of understanding in a mid-lesson answer. Keep it upstream — in the drafting — and the loop stays yours.

02 / 04

Human-in-the-loop marking

"AI marking" sounds like a machine handing back grades. Done well, it's the opposite: AI produces a fast, consistent first pass, and the teacher reviews, overrides, and owns every final decision.

The value isn't speed for its own sake — it's that a first-pass comment gives you something to react to instead of a blank page at 10pm. But a first pass is a draft, and drafts are frequently wrong: they miss context, misread a student's voice, and cannot weigh effort or growth. 03 / 04

Designing assessments AI can't do for the student

If a task can be completed by pasting the prompt into a chatbot, it was probably never assessing what you thought it was. The durable fix isn't detection software — it's better task design.

Detectors are unreliable and unfair: they flag second-language writers, they miss competent paraphrasing, and they turn the classroom into a courtroom. The stronger move is to redesign the task so that AI-generated text simply doesn't earn the marks.     Authentic assessment doesn't ban AI — it makes AI a starting point rather than a finish line. When the task demands the student's own voice, a specific classroom source, an in-person defence, or a visible process, a chatbot draft becomes raw material the student must still think with. The goal isn't to out-run the technology. It's to assess the things the technology can't fake: reasoning you can watch, choices a learner can justify, work that could only be theirs.

    Five levers that shift a task toward AI-resistant

    You don't have to redesign everything from scratch. Most tasks can be nudged along a spectrum by pulling one or two of these levers. None of them require detection software, and none of them assume students won't touch AI — they assume students will, and ask what remains genuinely theirs once they have.

    Lever 1

    Anchor to the local

    Tie the task to something only your classroom shares — a discussion you had, a local dataset, a source you studied together. A model that never sat in the room can't ground its answer in it.

    Lever 2

    Assess the process

    Grade the drafting log, the annotated plan, the version history — not just the final artefact. Thinking that happens in stages is far harder to outsource than a finished product.

    Lever 3

    Demand a defence

    A short viva, a live Q&A, a one-minute explanation. If a student can defend the reasoning out loud, it's theirs — whoever or whatever helped draft it.

    Lever 4

    Require a personal stance

    Ask for a position the student must argue, connected to their own experience or a choice they justify. Generic prompts get generic answers; specific stances need a specific thinker.

    Lever 5

    Make AI the subject

    Let students use AI openly, then critique its output — where it was wrong, biased, or shallow. This assesses judgement about the tool, which is a skill worth teaching anyway.

    Notice that every lever assesses something more valuable than the essay it replaces: grounded reasoning, visible process, spoken understanding, a defensible position, critical judgement. Designing out AI-vulnerability, done honestly, tends to make assessment better — more human, more revealing — not merely harder to cheat on. That's the quiet win of the authentic approach: the defensive move and the pedagogical one turn out to be the same move.

    04 / 04

    The honest limits

    Using AI in assessment responsibly means naming its failure modes out loud — to yourself, to colleagues, and to students — and building the workflow so those failures can't quietly become a grade.

    None of the techniques in this lesson survive contact with a teacher who trusts the machine's output. The four cautions below aren't fine print; they're the conditions that make everything else safe. Read them as design constraints on your own practice.

    Privacy

    Don't feed identifiable work

    A student's full essay, name attached, may be personal data. De-identify before pasting into any external tool, and check your school's policy before it leaves the building.

    Hallucination

    It invents with confidence

    AI will assert a fact, a citation, or a rubric criterion that sounds authoritative and is simply wrong. Every generated item and comment is a draft to verify, never a truth to trust.

    Bias

    It carries hidden slant

    Models can mark dialect, non-standard structure, or unfamiliar names more harshly. If AI touches a grade, a human must check that it hasn't imported an unfair pattern.

    Integrity

    Detectors aren't evidence

    "AI-detection" scores are probabilistic and frequently wrong. Never accuse a student on a detector's word. Design tasks that make the question moot instead.

    The one rule that ties it together

    Across the loop, the marking pass, and the task redesign, the same principle keeps recurring: AI drafts, the teacher decides. It is not a slogan — it is a division of labour. The machine handles the parts that are mechanical, repeatable, and reversible. You hold the parts that require knowing a specific human, weighing effort against outcome, and being answerable for the result. Keep that line bright and AI becomes a genuine assistant. Blur it, and you've quietly outsourced the one thing a teacher can't delegate.

    What to carry forward

    Assessment with AI is a workflow, not a shortcut. Let the machine draft items, structure rubrics, and produce first-pass comments — then review, override, and own every final judgement. Redesign tasks so that AI is a starting point students think with, not a finish line they hand in. And never let a machine's number, or a detector's score, become a decision you can't defend.
