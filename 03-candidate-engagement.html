Week 4 · AI for Recruiters · Lesson 03

Interview the evidence, not the vibe.

The most consequential thing a recruiter does isn't sourcing — it's judging a human being against a role, and doing it fairly. Unstructured, gut-feel interviews are where bias does its quietest work, and where two candidates get measured on different rulers. AI can help you build structure, keep questions consistent, and turn messy notes into evidence against criteria. What it cannot do is decide who is right for the job, or be trusted to read "tone" and "culture fit" as if those were facts. AI supports. The recruiter verifies, and decides.

01 / 04

Structure is the interview's fairness engine

Decades of hiring research point to one unglamorous conclusion: the single biggest lever on both prediction and fairness is structure. A structured interview asks every candidate the same job-related questions, scored against the same anchors, with evidence written down. That's it. It isn't a fancy technique — it's a discipline that stops each interviewer from quietly inventing their own test.

A structured interview is a chain, and each link is a place AI can help — and a place the recruiter must own. Click through the five stages below. Each shows what AI can usefully draft or organise, set against what only a human recruiter can decide. Notice how the work moves from job to question to score to evidence to scorecard, and how structure — not the AI — is what keeps every candidate on the same ruler.

Figure 1 · The structured-interview chain — AI's role vs the recruiter's

The lesson underneath the animation is blunt: structure is the biggest bias-reducer available to a recruiter, and it costs nothing but discipline. AI can accelerate every stage — drafting competency-based questions, formatting scoring anchors, organising written evidence — but it cannot supply the discipline. If you let it generate a different question set for each candidate, or skip the evidence step because the summary "sounds right," you've thrown away the very thing that makes the interview fair. AI builds the scaffold faster; you decide that the scaffold gets used the same way for everyone.

Structured vs unstructured — see the difference

"I just get a feel for people" is the most expensive sentence in recruiting. Gut-feel interviews feel insightful and are mostly measuring whether the candidate reminds the interviewer of themselves. Structured interviews feel mechanical and predict job performance far better — while treating candidates consistently enough to defend. The gap isn't a matter of taste. It's measurable.

Here's where AI genuinely helps: it lowers the effort cost of doing the structured thing. Drafting a job-related question bank, writing behavioural anchors for a scoring scale, keeping the same set in front of you for every candidate — that's exactly the kind of consistent, structured drafting a model is good at. The reason recruiters skip structure is usually that it's tedious to set up, not that they doubt it works. AI removes the tedium. It does not remove your responsibility to run the same fair interview for everyone and to judge the answers yourself.

03 / 04

From messy notes to evidence against criteria

Interview notes are usually a mess — half-sentences, arrows, a scribble you can't read by the afternoon. AI can tidy them into something legible in seconds, and that's a real convenience. But there's a trap hiding in the cleanup: a fluent model doesn't just organise your notes, it interprets them — and it will happily promote a vague impression like "good culture fit" into confident-sounding evidence it never had.

Below is a stepper. Start with the raw notes as jotted. Run the AI tidy-up and watch it produce clean prose — including some over-reading you must catch. Then map what's left to the actual job criteria, striking out anything that's an impression rather than evidence. The point isn't that AI is useless here; it's that the recruiter's job is to convert impressions into evidence-against-criteria, and to delete the model's confident guesses about tone and "fit." Candidate consent to notes and recordings, and human review of every summary, are not optional.

Consent + review required

Notes and recordings need the candidate's consent, and every AI summary needs a human read before it counts. This gate does not open until the recruiter has reviewed the evidence and removed the over-reading.

The most dangerous phrase an AI will hand you is "culture fit." It sounds like a finding; it is almost always a mirror. A model asked to summarise an interview will confidently describe a candidate as "energetic," "not quite our vibe," or "a strong cultural match" — reading tone it cannot verify and importing bias it cannot see. Strike those. Keep only what maps to a job-related criterion with an example behind it: "structured her answer around a real conflict she resolved" is evidence; "seemed like one of us" is not. The recruiter's discipline is the difference between a defensible record and a documented gut reaction.

How to ask AI for honest interview support

As in every lesson this week, the quality of the help depends on the framing of the request — and on refusing to let the tool overstep. These four patterns keep AI drafting the structure while you keep the judgement. No technical skill required; just clear instructions and a firm line.

Anchor to the job

Competency, not personality

"Draft five behavioural questions that test project prioritisation for a delivery-lead role." Ask for job-related competencies, never for questions that screen "fit," likeability, or background. The role defines the questions.

Ask for anchors

Make scoring consistent

Have AI draft behavioural anchors for a 1–4 scale so every interviewer scores the same answer the same way. Consistency across candidates is the fairness you're buying — and the thing you must protect.

Summarise, don't judge

Evidence, no verdict

"Summarise these notes as evidence against these criteria — do not infer personality, tone, or fit." Instruct the model to stay factual. Then strike anything that slipped through anyway.

De-identify

No candidate PII in tools

Never paste a candidate's name, contact details, or identifying facts into a consumer AI tool — it can breach GDPR and their equivalents. Use role and placeholders; the identity stays in your sanctioned system.

Even with a perfect prompt, the final read never moves. You check that the questions are job-related, that the scoring stayed consistent, that no impression was smuggled in as evidence — before any of it informs a decision about a person's livelihood. AI is fluent enough to make a biased summary sound objective, which is exactly why fluency can't be trusted as fairness. The last read is where the recruiter's judgement, and accountability, re-enter. It is not optional.

04 / 04

Engagement without automation replacing the human

Interviewing sits inside a wider engagement job: keeping candidates warm, answering their questions, treating them like people even when they won't get the role. AI can lift a lot of that load — but candidate experience is a relationship, and a relationship can't be fully outsourced to a template. Point AI at the drafting, keep yourself in the conversation.

The line is the same one running through the whole week: AI supports, the recruiter verifies and decides. Use it to draft the interview invite you'll personalise, to prepare a consistent question set you'll actually run, to structure feedback you'll deliver with care, to summarise a debrief you'll still lead. What it must never become is the thing that decides who advances, the reader of "tone" you defer to, or a reason to stop treating candidates as humans. The four uses below keep AI in the assistant's chair.

Invite

Draft, then personalise

Have AI draft a clear, warm interview invitation with the logistics and what to expect — then add the human touch and check every detail. A candidate can tell a template from a person, and it shapes how they see you.

Prepare

Build the question set

Ask AI to draft a consistent, job-related question bank with scoring anchors. Then commit to running the same set for every candidate. The preparation is where fairness is decided, long before the interview.

Debrief

Structure, then challenge

Use AI to organise interviewer notes into an evidence-based debrief format. Then make the panel argue from evidence, not impressions. The tidy summary is a starting frame, not a verdict on the person.

Respond

Timely, human feedback

Draft kind, specific feedback for candidates — including those you're rejecting — then review and send it yourself. Ghosting is a choice; so is treating a rejected candidate with respect. That choice is yours.

The honest limits, one more time

Everything in this lesson depends on the same guardrails. Candidate privacy: never put a candidate's identifiable data into a consumer AI tool — under GDPR and its equivalents, use de-identified prompts and keep identity in your sanctioned system. Bias & adverse impact: unstructured judgement and AI-read "tone" both import bias; structure and evidence-against-criteria are your defence, and you must watch for disparate impact across groups. Hallucination: AI will confidently invent a candidate's qualification, promote an impression into evidence, or read a "culture fit" it never observed — verify everything against what actually happened. Not the decision-maker: AI does not choose who advances, does not score the candidate, and does not judge "fit"; it is decision-support and drafting, never the hiring decision. AI supports. The recruiter verifies and decides. That doesn't change when the task is an interview instead of a job post — it matters more, because it is a person's livelihood, directly, on the other end.

What to carry forward

Interviewing is where fairness is won or lost, and where AI is most tempting to over-trust. Let AI build the structure — the question bank, the scoring anchors, the tidy notes — but keep the discipline yourself: same questions for everyone, evidence against criteria, no impression dressed up as a fact, and never a candidate's identity in a consumer tool. Structure is the biggest bias-reducer you have.

  • Structure is the fairness engine · same questions, same anchors, written evidence, for everyone
  • Structured beats gut-feel · it predicts better and treats candidates consistently
  • Convert impressions to evidence · strike "culture fit" and any tone AI can't verify
  • Consent + human review · notes need consent, every AI summary needs a human read
  • AI supports, the recruiter verifies and decides · it never chooses who advances

This lesson is professional education for recruiters on workflow and judgement. It is not legal, compliance, or HR advice, and not an endorsement of any specific tool. AI systems described here are drafting and decision-support aids, not autonomous hiring decision-makers, and must never make or replace a hiring decision. Always follow your organisation's policies, your jurisdiction's employment and privacy law (including GDPR and equivalents), obtain candidate consent for notes and recordings, and apply human review before any AI output informs a decision about a candidate.
