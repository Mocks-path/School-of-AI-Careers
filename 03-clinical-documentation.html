Week 3 · AI for Doctors · Lesson 03

You sign it, so you own every word.

Documentation is where medicine quietly loses its clinicians — the after-visit notes, the referral letters, the prior-authorisation forms, the coding, the inbox that never empties. This is exactly the drafting AI can accelerate. The prize isn't a note written by a machine; it's the minutes returned to the patient in front of you. But the moment your name goes on that note, it becomes your clinical record, your legal document, and your responsibility. AI drafts. The clinician verifies, edits, attests, and decides — always.

01 / 04

The documentation loop

A clinical note is not one act but a loop of them, and AI enters that loop at a specific place — never at the end. Understanding where the machine helps, and where the clinician's judgement is non-negotiable, is the whole discipline of documenting safely with AI.

Click through the loop below. AI can listen, structure, and draft — it turns a messy encounter into a first-pass note in seconds. But two stages belong to the clinician alone and cannot be delegated: the REVIEW, where you catch what the model got wrong, and the ATTEST gate, where your signature converts a draft into the medical and legal record. Notice how the loop is designed so nothing an AI produced can reach the chart without a human passing through that gate.

Figure 1 · Where AI enters — and where it must stop

The loop always closes at ATTEST — a human gate. An unreviewed AI draft is not a record; it is a liability waiting for your signature.

The temptation, on a heavy clinic day, is to let a fluent draft carry you straight from Capture to Attest — to sign what reads well without truly reading it. That is precisely the failure this loop is built to prevent. A note that reads smoothly can still contain an exam you never performed or a plan you never chose. The machine's fluency is not evidence of accuracy. Your review is the only thing that makes the record true.

02 / 04

The note review — catching what AI got wrong

This is the skill that separates safe AI-assisted documentation from a slow-motion error: reading an AI-drafted note not for whether it sounds right, but for whether it is true to the encounter you actually had. Ambient scribes and note generators produce confident, well-formatted prose — and confident, well-formatted mistakes.

It is also wrong in three specific, dangerous ways that a machine will not flag for you: a hallucinated exam finding the model added to sound complete, a laterality error (left vs right), and a copy-forward error carried in from an old note that no longer applies. Click any highlighted line in the draft above. The AI will not tell you these are wrong — that is exactly why the human review exists. Read the reasoning, then decide: fix it, or keep it if the record is actually correct.

Notice what the review is not: it is not rubber-stamping, and it is not trusting the model because it was right last time. Each of these errors is invisible to the AI that wrote it — a hallucinated lung finding looks identical to a real one in the model's output, and a laterality flip is a single word that changes which limb gets operated on. The clinician reading against their own memory of the encounter is the only detector that works. When you cannot reconcile a line with what actually happened, you correct it or you cut it. That judgement is the point of your training, and no scribe replaces it.

03 / 04

The attestation gate

Attestation is the hinge of the whole workflow — the exact instant a draft stops being a suggestion and becomes your sworn clinical record. So the design principle is simple and absolute: you cannot sign a note you have not reviewed. The gate stays locked until the human work is done.

Work the gate below. The signature control is locked, and it will not open on a timer or a shortcut — it opens only when you have confirmed, item by item, that you did the reviewing a real note demands. This mirrors how it must work in practice: convenience never unlocks attestation; only the clinician's confirmed review does. And the reminder underneath is the one to carry out of this lesson unchanged — you own every word you sign, whoever, or whatever, drafted it first.

You cannot sign until the note is reviewed. Confirm each item above — the gate does not open automatically, and no shortcut skips it.

Why so deliberate? Because attestation carries weight that outlives the clinic day. The note you sign guides the next clinician, defends or exposes you in review, and follows the patient through their care. "The AI wrote it" is not a defence anyone will accept, and it should not be — the point of a licensed professional in the loop is that a human took responsibility. Treat the signature as what it is: not the end of a chore, but the moment you personally stand behind everything above it.

04 / 04

The administrative inbox — same loop, everywhere

The documentation loop you just worked is not only for clinic notes. Referral letters, prior-authorisation requests, patient-instruction handouts, coding, the endless portal inbox — all of it is drafting AI can accelerate, and all of it runs on the same rule: AI drafts, you verify and decide, nothing goes out unread.

Point AI at the administrative load and the minutes add up fast. But the guardrails travel with the task. A referral letter with a hallucinated history misdirects a colleague. A coding suggestion the model invented becomes a compliance problem with your name on it. A patient handout with a wrong dose is a safety event. The four uses below keep AI in the drafting seat — accelerating the text, never authoring the decision.

Draft

Letters & referrals

Have AI turn your de-identified bullet points into a clean referral or discharge letter — then read every clinical fact against the chart before it goes out under your name.

Translate

Patient instructions

Ask AI to render a plan as plain-language after-visit instructions. You verify the dose, the timing, and the red-flag advice are exactly right — a wrong number here reaches the patient.

Suggest

Coding support

Let AI suggest codes from the documented encounter. You confirm each against what you actually did and documented. Suggested is not selected; the code you submit is your attestation too.

Triage text

Inbox drafts

Have AI draft replies to routine portal messages for your review. It never sends, never advises, and never triages on its own — a clinician approves every word that reaches a patient.

The honest limits, one more time

Everything in this lesson depends on the same guardrails. Patient privacy: never put identifiable patient data into a consumer AI tool — under HIPAA, GDPR, and their equivalents, de-identification comes first, and many tools are simply not permitted for protected health information at all. Hallucination: AI invents findings, histories, and codes in fluent, plausible prose, so every clinical fact is verified against the source. Bias & health equity: generated text can carry assumptions that read differently across populations — review for whom the phrasing serves and whom it may harm. Not a cleared device: a general-purpose AI scribe or chatbot is a drafting aid, not a regulated diagnostic tool; it is decision-support, never the decision-maker. AI drafts. The clinician verifies, attests, and decides. That is the rule from the first note to the last message of the day.

What to carry forward

Documentation and administrative work is where AI gives clinicians the most obvious time back — and where the human guardrails matter most, because a fluent draft is so easy to sign unread. Let AI capture and draft; do the review it cannot do; and treat attestation as the moment you personally stand behind every word, whoever drafted it first.
