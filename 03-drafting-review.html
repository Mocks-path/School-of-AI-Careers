Week 2 · AI for Lawyers · Lesson 03

The machine drafts. The lawyer decides.

Drafting and reviewing documents is where most legal AI value lives — and where the most careful judgement is required. A model can produce a serviceable first draft of a clause or surface the obligations buried in a fifty-page agreement in seconds. What it cannot do is bear the duty owed to a client, or know which risk actually matters in this matter. The workflow that keeps you safe is not a tool trick; it is a durable habit: AI proposes, you verify, you decide, you sign.

01 / 04

The drafting & review loop

Reliable legal AI work isn't a single prompt — it's a loop with defined stations, and at every station the division of labour is the same shape: the model accelerates the mechanical part, the lawyer owns the judgement part. Learn the loop once and it transfers to any tool.

Click through each station below. Notice that the model's column never contains the words decide, advise, or approve — those live only in the lawyer's column. This is not a limitation to work around; it is the design that keeps generated text from becoming unaccountable legal advice. The loop is deliberately circular: review sends you back to redraft, and nothing leaves the loop until a human finalises it.

The temptation with a fluent tool is to collapse the loop — to let a clean-looking draft skip straight from generation to filing. That collapse is exactly where malpractice lives. Fluency is not accuracy: a model will phrase a wrong governing-law clause or an invented citation as smoothly as a correct one. The loop exists to insert human verification precisely where the machine is most persuasive and least reliable.

02 / 04

Reviewing a contract, issue by issue

AI-assisted contract review is genuinely useful: point a model at an agreement and it can surface obligations, dates, and risk terms far faster than a first manual pass. But "surface" is not "decide" — every flag is a candidate for a lawyer to confirm or override, and the model's silence is never proof that nothing is there.

Work through the review below as the reviewing lawyer. For each item the AI flagged, you confirm the flag or override it with your own judgement. The stepper is honest about the failure mode that matters most: somewhere in this contract sits a real problem the model did not flag at all. Watching for the machine's misses — not just checking its hits — is the reviewing lawyer's actual job.

Two lessons live in this figure. First, an AI flag is a prompt for judgement, not a verdict — the model can mislabel a market-standard indemnity as high risk, and only a lawyer who knows the deal can right-size it. Second, and more dangerous, the model does not know what it did not see. An unlimited-liability exposure hidden in a defined term, an auto-renewal with a punishing notice window, a missing limitation of liability — these absences don't announce themselves. Reviewing with AI means reading for the gaps, because a confident summary of the wrong ninety-five percent is worse than no summary at all.

03 / 04

Redlining against a playbook

A playbook encodes a firm or client's negotiated positions — the fallbacks it will accept, the terms it never will. AI can redline a counterparty's draft against that playbook in seconds, proposing compliant rewrites. But the playbook is only as good as the human who wrote it, and the rewrite is only a proposal until a lawyer adopts it.

Toggle between the counterparty's risky clause and the playbook-compliant rewrite. The redline shows what a well-instructed model can suggest: tightening an uncapped indemnity, restoring a mutual limitation of liability, fixing a one-sided termination right. Read the reasoning underneath each — because in a real negotiation you don't accept a rewrite for looking cleaner. You accept it because you understand why the playbook demands it, and you can defend that position across the table.

Figure 3 · Playbook redline

Playbook redlining is where AI most convincingly impersonates a junior associate — and where you must resist trusting it like one. A model applies the playbook literally and blindly: it will happily "fix" a clause the parties deliberately negotiated as an exception, or miss that this counterparty's leverage makes a fallback position unrealistic. The rewrite is a first pass to react to, not a decision to ratify. The lawyer decides which battles this deal is worth, and owns every word that ends up in the executed agreement.

How to instruct a review well

The quality of an AI review is set almost entirely by the framing you give it. A vague "review this contract" yields a vague summary. A grounded request — role, standard, scope, output — yields flags you can actually act on. These four patterns hold regardless of which tool sits in front of you.

Set the standard

Name the yardstick

"Review as counsel for the buyer, against our standard playbook." Without a defined position, the model reviews against a bland average that serves no side of the deal.

Bound the scope

Say what to find

Ask for specific categories — obligations, dates, liability, IP, termination — not "issues." A named checklist surfaces more, and makes the model's misses easier to spot.

Demand citations

Point to the text

Require every flag to quote the exact clause it comes from. Un-cited flags are where hallucinated terms hide; a quote you can check is a flag you can trust.

Verify authorities

Never file unread

Any case, statute, or defined term the model cites gets confirmed against the source. Models invent plausible-sounding authorities; a fabricated citation is a sanctionable filing.

Even with a flawless instruction, the closing move never changes: you read the whole document yourself before it goes out. A model's summary is a map, not the territory. The obligations it lists are a starting point for your reading, never a substitute for it — and the signature at the bottom is yours, which means the responsibility is too.

04 / 04

The honest limits

Everything in this lesson rests on the same four guardrails. They are not friction to route around; they are what separates competent AI-assisted practice from a disciplinary complaint. None of them is optional, and each becomes more tempting to skip exactly when the tool feels most impressive.

Confidentiality

Privilege can't leak

Client facts pasted into a public tool may be stored or used to train. De-identify, or use a vetted, contractually-protected environment. A privilege waiver is not undoable.

Hallucination

Invented authorities

Models fabricate cases, statutes, and defined terms with total confidence. Every cited authority is verified against the source before it enters a draft, filing, or advice.

Bias

Skewed defaults

Generated clauses carry the assumptions of their training data — often favouring one side, one jurisdiction's norms, one drafting style. Read the phrasing for whose interest it quietly serves.

Ownership

The signature is yours

Every draft, redline, and review goes out under your name and your duty to the client. The final read, the final edit, and the final responsibility never delegate to the machine.

Hold these together and a clear rule emerges. The model is the fastest junior you have ever worked with, and the least accountable — tireless, fluent, and utterly unbothered by being wrong. That combination is a gift for drafting and a trap for deciding. Use it to generate, to surface, to accelerate; never to judge. AI drafts, the lawyer verifies and decides. That sentence is the whole of the workflow, and it does not bend because the output looked convincing.

What to carry forward

Drafting and review is where AI gives lawyers the most leverage and demands the most discipline. Let the model draft clauses, surface obligations, and propose redlines — then verify every authority, read for the gaps it missed, and keep the decision, the advice, and the signature firmly human.
