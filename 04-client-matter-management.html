Week 2 · AI for Lawyers · Lesson 04

Reclaim the hours that aren't lawyering.

A great deal of legal work is not law — it's the intake summaries, the client update letters, the chronologies, the billing narratives that eat the margins of the day. This is exactly the drafting AI accelerates. The prize isn't perfect prose from a machine; it's the hours returned to counsel, to strategy, to the client conversation that actually needs you. But every word still goes out under your duty to that client — and privilege, once waived, does not come back.

Two honest caveats. Second, reclaimed time is only a gift if you protect it. If every hour AI frees up gets refilled with more matters, you've bought faster burnout, not a better practice. The tool gives you a choice; the choice is yours.

02 / 04

The confidentiality firewall

The moment before you paste text into an AI tool is the most important moment in the whole workflow. Whatever crosses that line may leave your control — and for a lawyer, that line is not just privacy. It is confidentiality and privilege, duties you cannot quietly waive on a client's behalf.

Public AI tools are not a privileged drawer. Text you paste can be stored, processed on servers you don't control, and — depending on the service — used to improve the model. A client's name, a matter number, a settlement figure, a fact that identifies the dispute: none of it belongs in an unvetted chatbot, because disclosure to a third party can waive the privilege that protects it. The discipline is simple and non-negotiable: de-identify first, get a human sign-off, then send. De-identifying isn't just a compliance box — it usually makes the AI output better and safer to reuse. A prompt to "draft a firm but professional demand letter for an unpaid freight invoice, using placeholders for parties and amounts" gets you a sound, reusable structure you fill in yourself, without ever exposing the client or the matter. And the sign-off gate matters as much as the masking: automation is not permission. A person looks, a person confirms, a person is accountable. Always check your firm's data policy and your jurisdiction's confidentiality rules first — they may forbid certain tools entirely.

The plain-language tone tuner

The hardest client letters to write are rarely the longest — they're the ones where a dense legal draft has to become something a worried client can actually understand. AI can re-express a paragraph of legalese in plain language in seconds. What it can't do is know whether the simpler version is still legally accurate, or carry the duty for how the client relies on it.

Plain-language rewriting carries a specific danger: simplification can quietly change legal meaning. "You may be entitled to" is not "you are entitled to"; "without prejudice" is not decoration. A model chasing readability can smooth away the exact qualifier that protects the client — or you. Treat every plain-language draft as a first pass to check line-by-line against the legal substance, and treat any translation of a sensitive letter as a draft for a fluent human to verify, never a finished message. Convenience never outranks a client understanding precisely, and accurately, where their matter stands.

How to ask for a good draft

The difference between a useless AI draft and a genuinely helpful one is almost always the request. A one-line prompt gets a one-size-fits-nobody reply. A well-framed one gets a draft that's 80% there. These four patterns turn AI from a novelty into a reliable drafting partner for client communication — no technical skill required, just clear instructions.

Give it a role

Set the voice

"You're a solicitor writing a reassuring but precise update to a non-lawyer client." A defined role produces a defined tone — far better than a blank request that averages every letter ever written.

Set the frame

Audience, length, purpose

Say who reads it, how long it should be, and what it must achieve. "A short, plain-language note whose goal is to explain a delay without alarming the client" beats "write an update."

Feed the facts

Provide the raw material

Paste your points — de-identified — and let AI shape them. It's a rewriter, not a mind-reader. The facts, the legal position, and the advice come from you; the phrasing can come from it.

Iterate

Refine, don't restart

"Plainer." "Keep the qualifier in the second sentence." "Shorter." Treat the first draft as a conversation, not a verdict. Two or three nudges usually beats re-prompting from scratch.

Even with a perfect prompt, the last step never changes: you read the whole thing, checking it still says exactly what the law requires, before it leaves your hands. AI is fluent, which is exactly what makes it dangerous — it can phrase a subtly wrong legal statement so smoothly that you nod past the error. Fluency is not accuracy. The final read is where your professional judgement and your duty to the client re-enter, and it is not optional.

04 / 04

Matter management & professional growth

The same drafting power that clears your non-billable load can sharpen your practice — if you point it at organisation, reflection, and learning rather than just output. Used well, AI becomes a tireless junior and a thinking partner. Used lazily, it becomes a way to stop doing the reading, and to stop thinking.

The line is the same one running through this whole lesson: AI proposes, you verify and decide. Use it to build the chronology you'll then check against the record, to draft the matter summary you'll make accurate, to summarise research you'll still read in the source, to rehearse a difficult client conversation before you have it. What it must never become is the author of your legal judgement or the keeper of your obligations. The four uses below keep AI in the assistant's chair, where it belongs.

Organise

Draft the chronology

Have AI assemble a first chronology or matter summary from de-identified notes — then check every date and fact against the actual record. A wrong date the model invented is your error to catch.

Reflect

Structure, then get honest

Ask AI to scaffold a matter post-mortem or a self-review. Then rewrite it as what actually happened. The machine's tidy version is a starting frame, not the truth of the work.

Digest

Summarise, then verify

Have AI condense a judgment or a long policy into key points — then read the source on anything that matters. Summaries drop nuance, misstate holdings, and sometimes invent them.

Rehearse

Role-play the hard talk

Practise a difficult client or partner conversation with AI playing the other side. Low stakes to rehearse; the real conversation, and the advice in it, is still yours to lead and own.

The honest limits, one more time

Everything in this lesson depends on the same guardrails. Confidentiality & privilege: never put client-identifying or privileged material into an unvetted tool — a waiver is not undoable. Hallucination: AI states wrong facts, dates, and authorities confidently, so verify everything against the source. Bias: generated tone and phrasing carry assumptions — read for whose interest they quietly serve. Ownership: every letter, chronology, and billing narrative goes out under your name and your duty to the client, which means the final read, the final edit, and the final responsibility are yours too. AI drafts. The lawyer verifies and decides. That doesn't change when the task is a client letter instead of a contract — it just matters even more, because the machine's convenience is at its most tempting exactly here.

What to carry forward

Client communication and matter management is where AI gives lawyers the most obvious time back — and where the human guardrails matter most, because the machine's convenience is most tempting. Let AI draft the intake summaries, client letters, chronologies, and billing narratives; de-identify anything privileged before it crosses the line; and keep a human sign-off on everything that carries your name and your duty.
