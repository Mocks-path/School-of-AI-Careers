Week 3 · AI for Doctors · Lesson 04

Give the minutes back to the patient.

Much of what exhausts a clinician isn't medicine — it's the after-visit summaries, the portal messages, the letters, the forms that fill the pyjama-time hours after the clinic closes. This is exactly the drafting AI can accelerate, and the payoff is real: minutes returned to sleep, to family, to the patient conversation that actually needs you. But every word that reaches a patient still carries your clinical duty — and no patient's identifiable data belongs in a consumer chatbot. AI drafts. The clinician verifies, and decides.

The tool gives you a choice; the choice is yours.

02 / 04

The privacy firewall

The moment before you paste text into an AI tool is the most important moment in the whole workflow. Whatever crosses that line may leave your control — and in medicine, that line is protected by law. Identifiable patient information does not belong in a consumer AI tool, full stop.

Public AI tools are not part of your protected record. Text you paste can be stored, processed on servers you don't control, and — depending on the service — used to improve the model. A patient's name, date of birth, medical-record number, address, a rare diagnosis that identifies them: none of it belongs in an unvetted chatbot, because that disclosure can breach HIPAA, GDPR, and their equivalents, and cannot be undone. The discipline is simple and non-negotiable: de-identify first, get a human sign-off, then send. A clinician must confirm the text carries no patient-identifying detail before it goes to the tool — and must review the AI output again before anything reaches the patient. This gate does not open automatically.

De-identifying isn't just a compliance box — it usually makes the AI output better and safer to reuse. A prompt to "write a warm, plain-language after-visit summary for a patient newly diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, using placeholders for name and details" gets you a sound, reusable structure you fill in yourself, without ever exposing the patient. And the sign-off gate matters as much as the masking: automation is not permission. A clinician looks, a clinician confirms, a clinician is accountable — both before text goes to the tool and before the result reaches the patient. Always check your institution's data policy and your jurisdiction's privacy law first; many tools are simply not sanctioned for protected health information at all.

The plain-language health-literacy tuner

The hardest patient messages to write are rarely the longest — they're the ones where a dense clinical explanation has to become something a worried patient can actually understand and act on. AI can re-express a paragraph of jargon at a patient-friendly reading level in seconds. What it can't do is know whether the simpler version is still clinically accurate and safe — or carry the duty for how the patient relies on it.

Plain-language rewriting carries a specific danger: simplification can quietly change clinical meaning. "Take with food if it upsets your stomach" is not "take with food"; "call us if it gets worse" is not the same as a defined red-flag warning. A model chasing readability can smooth away the exact safety qualifier that protects the patient. Treat every plain-language draft as a first pass to check line-by-line against the clinical substance, and treat any translation of medical instructions as a draft for a fluent human to verify, never a finished message. And hold the hard line: an AI tool does not triage symptoms, does not diagnose, and does not give the advice of record. It shapes words you have already decided are safe. Convenience never outranks a patient understanding — accurately and safely — what to do next.

How to ask for a good draft

The difference between a useless AI draft and a genuinely helpful one is almost always the request. A one-line prompt gets a one-size-fits-nobody reply. A well-framed one gets a draft that's 80% there. These four patterns turn AI from a novelty into a reliable drafting partner for patient communication — no technical skill required, just clear instructions.

Give it a role

Set the voice

"You're a clinician writing a reassuring, plain-language note to a worried patient." A defined role produces a defined tone — far better than a blank request that averages every message ever written.

Set the frame

Audience, level, purpose

Say who reads it, at what reading level, and what it must achieve. "A short note at a grade-six reading level explaining a new diabetes diagnosis without alarming the patient" beats "write a summary."

Feed the facts

Provide the raw material

Paste your points — de-identified — and let AI shape them. It's a rewriter, not a mind-reader. The diagnosis, the plan, and the safety advice come from you; the phrasing can come from it.

Iterate

Refine, don't restart

"Simpler." "Keep the red-flag warning exactly as written." "Shorter." Treat the first draft as a conversation, not a verdict. Two or three nudges usually beats re-prompting from scratch.

Even with a perfect prompt, the last step never changes: you read the whole thing, checking it is still clinically accurate and safe, before it leaves your hands. AI is fluent, which is exactly what makes it dangerous — it can phrase a subtly wrong instruction so smoothly that you nod past the error. Fluency is not accuracy. The final read is where your clinical judgement and your duty to the patient re-enter, and it is not optional.

04 / 04

Practice management & professional growth

The same drafting power that clears your communication load can sharpen your practice — if you point it at organisation, reflection, and learning rather than just output. Used well, AI becomes a tireless assistant and a thinking partner. Used lazily, it becomes a way to stop reading the evidence, and to stop thinking.

The line is the same one running through this whole week: AI proposes, you verify and decide. Use it to draft the patient-education handout you'll then check for safety, to structure a quality-improvement reflection you'll make honest, to summarise a guideline you'll still read in the source, to rehearse a difficult conversation before you have it. What it must never become is the author of your clinical judgement, the keeper of your obligations, or a stand-in for a colleague, a specialist, or your own reading. The four uses below keep AI in the assistant's chair, where it belongs.

Organise

Draft patient education

Have AI draft a de-identified education handout or FAQ — then check every dose, instruction, and red-flag against guidance before it reaches a patient. A wrong number the model invented is your error to catch.

Reflect

Structure, then get honest

Ask AI to scaffold a reflective practice entry or QI write-up. Then rewrite it as what actually happened. The machine's tidy version is a starting frame, not the truth of the work.

Digest

Summarise, then verify

Have AI condense a long guideline or paper into key points — then read the source on anything that changes management. Summaries drop nuance, misstate findings, and sometimes invent them.

Rehearse

Role-play the hard talk

Practise breaking difficult news or a boundary conversation with AI playing the other side. Low stakes to rehearse; the real conversation, and the care in it, is still yours to lead and own.

The honest limits, one more time

Everything in this lesson depends on the same guardrails. Patient privacy: never put identifiable patient data into a consumer AI tool — under HIPAA, GDPR, and their equivalents, de-identification comes first, and many tools are not permitted for protected health information at all. Hallucination: AI invents doses, instructions, and facts in fluent, plausible prose, so verify everything against the source before it reaches a patient. Bias & health equity: generated tone and phrasing carry assumptions that land differently across languages, cultures, and literacy levels — review for whom the message serves and whom it may leave behind. Not a cleared device: a general-purpose chatbot does not triage, does not diagnose, and does not give advice of record; it is decision-support and drafting, never the decision-maker. AI drafts. The clinician verifies and decides. That doesn't change when the task is a patient message instead of a clinic note — it just matters even more, because it is the patient, directly, on the other end.

What to carry forward

Patient communication and practice management is where AI gives clinicians the most obvious time back — and where the human guardrails matter most, because the words reach a patient directly. Let AI draft the summaries, messages, letters, and handouts; de-identify anything before it crosses the line; keep a human review before anything reaches a patient; and never let a tool triage or advise in your place.
