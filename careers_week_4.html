Chapter One · The Req That Won't Wait

Set Up the AI Workflow & the Fairness Ground Rules

Before Sam asks AI for a single thing, they decide what AI is for — decision support, never the hiring decision — and draw the bright lines they will not cross: protect candidate privacy, no auto-reject (a human sees every no), and verify everything before it touches a candidate.

📖 The story so far Monday, 8:40 a.m. The req has been open for six weeks, the hiring manager wants a shortlist "yesterday," and 180 applications are sitting in the inbox. A peer swears an AI screener "cut her sourcing time in half," but Sam has read the news too: a resume tool that quietly downranked candidates from certain schools, a chatbot that invented a certification a candidate never held, a firm that pasted whole resumes into a consumer app. So before Sam types anything, they do the professional thing: they set fairness ground rules first, and use AI to lighten the workload — not to replace their judgment or their duty to treat every candidate fairly.
⚖️

AI supports, I decide

AI drafts, structures, and summarises in seconds; Sam reads every line and owns the call. It is decision-support, never the hire, reject, or shortlist — Sam's name is on the outcome, and the candidate deserves a human's judgment.

🔒

No candidate PII in consumer tools

No names, contact details, or full resumes into a general AI app whose data handling Sam can't control. They work with de-identified facts and placeholders, never a real person's identity.

🚫

No auto-reject — a human sees every no

AI may sort or flag, but it never sends a rejection on its own. Every adverse decision — every "not moving forward" — is reviewed and owned by a person before it reaches a candidate.

🔍

Verify & watch for bias

Every qualification, claim, and AI-suggested criterion gets checked against reality and against fairness — no proxies for protected traits, no invented credentials. AI sounds confident even when it's biased or wrong.

Chapter Two · From Fuzzy Role to Fair Criteria

Turn the Role into Fair Criteria — and Screen Without Bias

AI can help Sam turn a vague role into structured, job-related criteria and an inclusive job description in minutes — but it can also bake in a proxy for a protected trait that looks neutral, and hallucinate a qualification a candidate never actually held. So Sam treats every AI-suggested criterion as unverified until they've checked it's job-related and fair, and every candidate claim as unverified until confirmed. This chapter's mini-game: catch the proxy bias and the fabricated qualification before they reach a shortlist.

📖 The story so far Monday, 11:20 a.m. The hiring manager's brief is a paragraph of adjectives: "a rockstar who's a great culture fit and hits the ground running." Sam asks AI to turn that into concrete, job-related criteria and an inclusive job description — a reasonable use of a fast assistant. It returns a tidy criteria list and a polished JD. Old habits would ship it straight to the job board. New Sam does the one thing that separates a careful recruiter from a lawsuit and a missed great hire: they check every criterion for hidden bias, and verify every candidate claim against reality before trusting a word. Chapter Three · Evidence, Not Vibes

Build a Structured Interview Guide — and Turn Notes into Evidence

A structured interview — same job-related questions, a clear scorecard, every candidate scored the same way — is the single strongest tool a recruiter has for fair, comparable hiring. AI can draft the guide and turn Sam's messy notes into clean evidence in seconds — but Sam keeps their hands on the wheel: keep, fix, or reject every summarised note before it counts. The trap this chapter teaches: AI loves to over-read "culture fit," tone, and personality — reading bias into a candidate's demeanour instead of sticking to job-related evidence.

📖 The story so far Tuesday, 2:00 p.m. The interviews are done. Sam asks AI to turn three pages of scribbled notes into a clean scorecard summary — genuinely a time-saver. Most of it is accurate and job-related. But two lines are wrong in ways that matter: AI wrote that a candidate "wasn't a culture fit — seemed a bit reserved and formal" (over-reading demeanour and tone, not job-related evidence), and it recorded a skill score AI simply inferred that Sam never actually assessed in the interview. AI drafts; Sam reviews against what the candidate actually demonstrated, and catches the vibe-reading and the invented score — before it counts.
📕 Sam's Scorecard-Review Checklist · what they check before a note becomes evidence (the human layer AI can't replace)
What to check
Sam's standard
Evidence, not vibes
Every rating must rest on something the candidate actually said or demonstrated against a job-related question — not their tone, accent, confidence, or "energy." A judgment of demeanour is bias to delete, not evidence to keep.
"Culture fit" is a trap
"Culture fit" too often means "reminds me of me." Sam replaces it with concrete, job-related values-in-action questions everyone answers the same way. AI over-reads reserved, formal, or unfamiliar styles as red flags — Sam doesn't.
Scored, not inferred
A score exists only for a competency Sam actually asked about and observed. AI will confidently fill a gap with a guess. An un-assessed skill gets no number — it gets a follow-up, not a fabrication.
Same bar for everyone
The scorecard means the same questions, same rubric, same standard for every candidate. That's what makes it fair and comparable. Sam never lets AI's summary quietly move the bar for one person.
💡 Why a scorecard? A structured scorecard is Sam's memory of what "fair and comparable" looks like, written down. AI drafts against the words it heard; the scorecard lets Sam review against what the candidate actually demonstrated and what the job actually needs. AI produced a summary; the scorecard is what turns messy notes into fair, defensible evidence.

Chapter Four · Behind the Privacy Firewall

Run a Fair Debrief & Send a Kind, Human-Reviewed Decision

The debrief and the candidate communication are where AI saves the most time — and where a careless paste can expose candidate personally identifiable information (PII), and where an auto-sent rejection can hurt a real person. Sam's trick: run an evidence-based debrief off the scorecards, draft candidate messages with placeholders and de-identified facts behind a privacy firewall, then fill in the real name and details themselves, inside their secure system — and let no adverse decision go out without a human's eyes on it.

📖 The story so far Wednesday, 4:30 p.m. Decisions need making and candidates need answers — kindly and promptly. The temptation is huge: paste the whole candidate list, names, emails, and full resumes into a consumer AI app and say "rank these and auto-send rejections to the bottom half." Sam doesn't. They run the debrief on the evidence — the structured scorecards — not on gut feel or the loudest voice in the room. They build a privacy firewall: AI never sees a real name, email, or identifiable detail. It drafts warm, clear message scaffolding; Sam pours the real candidate information in afterward, inside their secure system, and personally reviews every "no" before it sends. Same time saved, zero PII exposed, no one auto-rejected.
🚫 The tempting shortcut (never do this)

"Rank my candidates and write rejections for Amara Nwosu (amara.n@email.com, +1-555-0148), 14 Rosewood Lane — her resume is pasted below with her date of birth and current employer — and auto-send the 'no' emails to the bottom half."

Real name, email, phone, address, date of birth, employer, and a full resume — pasted into a general consumer tool whose data handling you don't control, plus an automated rejection with no human review. That's candidate PII leaving your secure system, and an adverse decision made by a machine.

✅ The privacy firewall (do this)

"Draft a warm, respectful 'not moving forward this time' message for [CANDIDATE] who reached the final interview stage. Be kind, specific to the stage they reached, and encourage future applications. Keep all names, contact details, and specifics as placeholders."

AI writes the scaffolding from a stage, not an identity. Sam fills [CANDIDATE] in themselves, inside the secure applicant system, and reads every decision before it sends. Fast, kind, and safe.

Lesson 1

AI Workflow & Fairness Ground Rules

AI supports, you verify and decide. Bright lines: never put candidate PII in consumer tools, no auto-reject (a human sees every no), and AI must not make the hiring call — it's decision-support, not a decision-maker. Chapter 1.

Lesson 2

Fair Criteria & Bias-Aware Screening

AI turns a fuzzy role into criteria and an inclusive JD fast — but it can bake in a proxy for a protected trait and invent a qualification. Screen every criterion for hidden bias; verify every claim in a real source. Chapter 2.

Lesson 3

Structured Interviews & Evidence

A scorecard makes hiring fair and comparable; AI turns messy notes into evidence. Keep, fix, or reject every line — and catch what AI over-reads as "culture fit" or a score it invented. Chapter 3.

Lesson 4

Fair Debriefs & Kind Decisions

Debrief on evidence, draft with placeholders and a stage, not names and resumes. AI shapes warm, timely message tone; you fill in details inside your secure system and review every no. Zero PII exposed. Chapter 4.

Sam Okafor is a teaching scenario, and nothing here is legal or employment-law advice — candidates, criteria, and credentials in this story are illustrative placeholders, not recommendations.
