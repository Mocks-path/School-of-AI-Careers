The AI-Augmented Teacher — AI drafts, the teacher decides

Three hands-on activities. Sort real classroom tasks into what AI can safely draft, what only you can decide, and what should never touch a chatbot. Then pin down the one pattern that keeps AI useful and honest — and reveal where it helps across your five core workflows.

Progress
1

Sort the task — draft it, decide it, or never touch it?

Not every classroom task belongs near an AI tool. The useful question is not "can AI do this?" but "what is at stake if it gets this wrong?" Three buckets, three risk levels. Study the cards, then drag each task where it belongs. Guess freely — wrong pills carry a hover hint with the answer.

AI

Great for AI — draft

Low-stakes, high-volume first drafts you will read and edit anyway. AI speeds the blank page; your judgment finishes the job.

+ Saves the slow "blank page" minutes + Offers variety you can pick from − Always verify facts, tone, and fit − A draft is never the final word
YOU

Human-only — decide

Judgment calls that define a child's experience: grades that count, relationships, wellbeing, professional accountability. AI can inform, never decide.

+ You hold the context AI cannot see + You are accountable, so you decide − AI has no duty of care − Outsourcing judgment erodes trust
NO

Never — privacy risk

Anything that would paste a named child's private data into a public tool: medical notes, safeguarding, home situations, identifiable records.

+ Protects pupils by default + Keeps you inside data-protection rules − Convenience is never worth the breach − "Anonymise first" is not always enough
💡 The rule of thumb: if a mistake would only cost you a minute of editing, AI can draft it. If a mistake would harm a relationship or a grade, you decide it. If a mistake would expose a child's private life, it never goes in.

The honest part — what AI gets wrong, and how you guard against it

Being augmented does not mean being uncritical. Three failure modes show up in every teaching tool, no matter how polished. Knowing them — and the simple guard for each — is what separates a professional using AI from a person trusting a chatbot.

Hallucination Invents facts

AI will state made-up dates, fake quotes, and official-sounding standards that do not exist — confidently, in fluent prose. It has no live link to your curriculum and fills gaps with plausible fiction.

Guard: verify every fact, date, and cited standard against a real source before it reaches a pupil. If you can't verify it, don't use it.

Bias Skews content

Trained on human text, AI can reproduce stereotypes — in the names it picks, the examples it reaches for, whose stories it centres, and which reading it treats as "normal". It won't flag this for you.

Guard: read every draft for who is represented and who is missing. You know your class and community; adjust names, examples, and framing to fit real children.

Privacy Leaks data

Text you paste into a public tool can be stored and used to train future models. A named pupil's grades, diagnosis, or home situation entered "just to get help" is a data-protection breach.

Guard: never enter identifiable pupil data. Work in generic terms, keep the personal detail in your own head, and add it back during your edit.
💡 Put together, these are one habit: treat AI output as a confident intern's first draft — fast and helpful, never trusted unread. You verify the facts, you check who's represented, and you keep pupils' data out. That is the whole discipline of the augmented teacher.

Lesson 1 complete — what you now carry

›Sort by stakes, not by capability. Ask what a mistake would cost before you ask whether AI can do the task.
›AI drafts, the teacher decides. The tool proposes; you own the judgment, the grade, and the relationship.
›Privacy is a hard line. Named pupils and sensitive data never go into public tools — convenience never wins that trade.
›Be honest about limits. AI hallucinates, carries bias, and cannot see your classroom — verification is part of the workflow, not an extra.
