Week 1 · AI for Teachers

Assessment & Feedback with AI — AI drafts, the teacher decides

Three hands-on activities. Order the assessment loop from goal to adjustment, sit in the marker's chair and decide whether to accept or override an AI's first-pass grade, then sort tasks by how easily AI can game them — and redesign the weak ones into authentic assessment. You'll write no code. You'll practise judgement.

Progress
“

The through-line for this hour: AI can draft rubrics, generate items, and suggest feedback in seconds — but it cannot know your student, your context, or your professional standard. Every activity below keeps a human decision at the point where it matters. AI proposes; you dispose.

2

The marker's chair — accept or override?

Letting AI mark unsupervised is where teachers get burned: it's confident, fast, and sometimes flatly wrong. The durable skill is reviewing an AI first pass and deciding — with justification — whether to accept it, edit it, or throw it out. Read each student response, read the AI's draft grade and comment, then choose the professional move.

3

AI-resistant or easily gamed?

A student with a chatbot can complete some tasks in ten seconds — and learn nothing. Others resist that shortcut because they demand process, personal context, or live thinking. Study the two profiles, sort each assessment task into its bucket, then pick the authentic redesign that turns a gameable task into a meaningful one.

R

AI-resistant

Hard to fake because the value is in the doing, not the artefact.

  • Requires in-class or oral demonstration
  • Ties to the student's own data, draft, or experience
  • Assesses process: notes, revisions, reasoning aloud
  • Asks "why did you choose this?" not just "what is the answer?"
G

Easily gamed

A generic prompt-and-paste produces a passable answer with no learning.

  • Take-home essay on a common, public topic
  • Definition or summary of a well-known text
  • Answer has one findable "correct" form
  • No process, no context, no defence required

What you can now do

You didn't learn a tool — you rehearsed three judgement calls that hold up no matter which AI you use next year.

  • ✓ Run the loop: Define → Format → Items → Rubric → Mark → Feedback → Adjust — and know that AI helps most in the middle, never at the ends you own.
  • ✓ Mark with a human in the loop: treat every AI grade and comment as a draft to verify against the rubric and the actual student, not a verdict.
  • ✓ Design for integrity: spot easily-gamed tasks and redesign them around process, personal context, and live defence.
  • ✓ Stay honest about the risks: AI hallucinates confident wrong feedback, can carry bias into grading, and mishandles student data — so the teacher's review is the safeguard, not an optional extra.

Quick reflect — make it stick

A sentence each is enough. Writing it in your own words is what moves it into practice. (Nothing is saved or sent.)

Think of one assessment you set this term that would be "easily gamed" by a chatbot. What single change would make it AI-resistant without doubling your workload?
When you use AI to draft feedback, what will you always check before it reaches a student — and why is that check non-negotiable?
