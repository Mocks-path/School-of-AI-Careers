Good research is framing plus verification

AI research tools are fast, fluent, and sometimes confidently wrong. Two disciplines make them safe to use: give the tool enough frame to answer the real question, and verify every authority it returns against a primary source before you rely on it. The rest of this lesson drills both.

Q

Frame the question

A vague query returns vague — or invented — answers. Jurisdiction, area of law, the material facts, procedural posture, and what you actually need turn a prompt into a brief.

✓

Verify the authority

Every case, statute, and quotation is checked in an authoritative primary source. If you cannot pull it up and read it, you do not cite it — no exceptions.

You

Own the analysis

AI can surface leads and draft structure. Applying the law to the facts, and deciding what the answer is, remains the lawyer's professional judgment.

💡 The through-line from Lesson 1: AI drafts the research; the lawyer verifies and decides. In research, "verify" has a precise meaning — read the actual authority in a primary source.

The honest part — how AI research goes wrong

Legal research is where AI's failure modes bite hardest, because a fabricated authority can end up in a court filing. Three risks, three guards — the discipline that keeps you on the right side of your professional duties.

Fabricated cases Invents authority

AI invents case names, citations, and holdings that read like real law and fit your argument perfectly — which is exactly why they are dangerous. Lawyers have faced sanctions for citing cases that never existed.

Guard: pull every cited case and statute in an authoritative source and read it yourself. If it will not come up, it is not real — do not cite it.

Outdated or overruled law Stale answers

Even a real case may have been overruled, distinguished, or superseded by statute since the model's training data. AI often will not know it is quoting law that no longer governs.

Guard: confirm each authority is current and good law before you rely on it, using an up-to-date primary source or citator — not the model's word.

Confidentiality in the prompt Leaks the matter

To get a "better" answer it is tempting to paste the real facts, party names, and documents into a public tool — which can breach confidentiality and risk waiving privilege.

Guard: frame the question in generic, hypothetical terms. Give the tool the legal shape of the problem, never the identifying detail of the matter.
💡 One habit ties them together: nothing an AI research tool tells you is authority until you have read it yourself in a primary source and confirmed it is current — and you got there without handing over the client's confidential matter. That is verification, and it is the whole job.

Lesson 2 complete — what you now carry

›Frame before you search. Jurisdiction, area of law, material facts, procedural posture, and what you need turn a vague query into a brief AI can answer — without leaking confidential detail.
›Verify every authority. Cases, statutes, and quotations are read in a primary source and confirmed current. A citation you cannot pull up does not exist.
›Verification sits before synthesis. Issue → Frame → Search → VERIFY → Synthesise → Apply. Checking after you have built on the answer is too late.
›Watch for the fabricated case. The most convincing AI output is the most dangerous — a perfect-fit authority that never existed. Catch it in verification, every time.
