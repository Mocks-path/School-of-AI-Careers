Drafting & Document Review — AI drafts, the lawyer verifies and decides

Three hands-on activities built around real legal judgement. First, order the drafting-and-review loop so speed never skips verification. Then take the reviewer's chair: weigh what AI flagged in a contract and decide, with justification, what to accept and what to override — including the issue it missed and the one it over-flagged. Finally, sort clauses against a playbook and pick the redline you'd actually send.

The reviewer's chair

Playbook-compliant, or risky?

Most firms review against a playbook — a standard of what's acceptable and what isn't. AI can pre-sort clauses against it, but the standard is set by lawyers, and the close calls are yours. For each clause pulled from a vendor's draft, decide whether it's playbook-compliant (acceptable as written) or risky (needs a redline). Where AI speeds you up — and where it can't stand in for you

Drafting and review are exactly where generative AI feels most magical — and most dangerous. The honest picture: it's a superb first-drafter and a tireless first-pass reader, and a terrible final authority. Know which is which.

+

Where AI genuinely earns its keep

High-volume, low-final-authority work — draft fast, then verify.

  • A first draft of a standard clause or a routine letter you'll edit
  • Summarising a long document so you know where to read closely
  • Surfacing candidate issues and inconsistencies for you to weigh
  • Turning your bullet points into structured, formatted prose
–

Where the lawyer is the point

Authority, accuracy, and duty of care never delegate.

  • Verifying every citation, statute, and defined term against source
  • Deciding what risk your client can and can't accept
  • Catching the issue the model never flagged
  • Signing your name to advice you haven't independently checked
→

The honest caveats you carry into every AI draft: models invent citations and case law that don't exist, quietly misstate terms, and inherit bias from their training data. Anything touching a client's confidential facts may be stored by a public tool and could threaten privilege. So: never trust an authority you haven't pulled yourself, never paste unprotected client detail, and never let a fast draft substitute for your own read.
