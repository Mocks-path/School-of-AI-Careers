The AI-Augmented Clinician — AI suggests, the clinician verifies and decides

Three hands-on activities. Sort real clinical tasks into what AI can safely draft or support, what only a clinician can judge, and what must never touch a consumer tool. Then lock in the one pattern that keeps AI useful and safe — including how to catch a hallucinated reference and how decision-support differs from autonomy — and reveal where AI helps across your five core clinical workflows.

Progress
1

Sort the task — support it, judge it, or never touch it?

Not every clinical task belongs near an AI tool. The useful question is not "can AI do this?" but "what is at stake if it gets this wrong — a wasted edit, a patient's safety, or a breach of privacy?" Three buckets, three risk levels. Study the cards, then drag each task where it belongs. Guess freely — wrong pills carry a hover hint with the answer.

AI

Great for AI — draft & support

Low-stakes, high-volume first drafts and background support you will read and verify anyway: de-identified summaries, plain-language explanations, documentation scaffolds, patient-education handouts. AI beats the blank page; your judgment finishes the job.

+ Clears the slow "blank page" minutes + Offers structure and phrasing to refine − Always verify facts, doses, and references − A draft is never charted or acted on as-is
YOU

Clinician-only — judgment

Calls that define a patient's diagnosis and care: the diagnosis of record, the treatment plan, whether to escalate, what a physical exam reveals. AI can inform, never decide — and never bears the duty owed to the patient.

+ You hold the duty and the accountability + You see the patient AI cannot examine − AI has no professional or ethical duty − Outsourcing judgment risks the patient
NO

Never — PHI in consumer tools OR autonomous diagnosis

Anything that would paste identifiable patient data into a consumer tool — names, dates, record numbers, images that identify — or let AI issue the diagnosis or care decision on its own.

+ Protects patient privacy and trust + Keeps a qualified human accountable − A single paste can breach confidentiality − Consumer tools are not cleared devices
💡 The rule of thumb: if a mistake would only cost you a minute of editing, AI can draft or support it. If a mistake would harm a patient's diagnosis or care, you judge it. If it would expose identifiable patient data to a consumer tool, or let AI diagnose on its own, it never goes in.

The pattern that keeps AI safe — "AI suggests, the clinician verifies & decides"

One durable habit outlasts every tool and update: AI produces a suggestion; you verify it against the patient and trusted sources and decide. Most of a clinician's week runs through five repeating workflows. The honest part — what AI gets wrong, and how you guard against it

Being augmented does not mean being uncritical. Four failure modes show up in clinical AI tools, no matter how fluent. Knowing them — and the simple guard for each — is what separates a professional using AI from someone who trusted a chatbot at a patient's expense.

Hallucinated evidence Invents findings

AI will state made-up study names, fake citations, doses, and guideline quotations that were never written — confidently, in fluent clinical prose. It can also invent a "finding" a scan never showed.

Guard: verify every reference, dose, and guideline claim in an authoritative primary source before you rely on it. If you cannot pull it up and read it, treat it as fiction.

Patient privacy Leaks PHI

Text and images pasted into a consumer tool can be stored and used to train future models. Names, dates, record numbers, or identifiable scans entered "just to get help" can breach confidentiality.

Guard: never enter identifiable patient data into a consumer tool. Work in de-identified, generic terms; keep the identifiers out and add them back in your own note.

Bias & health equity Skews care

Trained on human data, AI can reproduce bias — under-serving groups under-represented in its training, echoing outdated race-based assumptions, or mis-weighting risk. It will not flag this for you.

Guard: read every output for whose data it was built on and what it assumes about this patient. Apply your own judgment to any risk score or recommendation about a person.

Non-cleared-device limits Not a diagnostic

A consumer chatbot is not a regulator-cleared diagnostic device. It has no validated accuracy for diagnosis, no accountability, and no place as the diagnostician of record.

Guard: use AI as decision-support only. The diagnosis and plan of record are made and signed by a qualified clinician, never issued autonomously by a consumer tool.
💡 Put together, these are one habit: treat AI output as a confident trainee's first draft — fast and helpful, never trusted unread. You verify every reference and dose against a primary source, you check the framing and equity, you keep identifiable data out of consumer tools, and the decision stays with you. That is the whole discipline of the augmented clinician.

Lesson 1 complete — what you now carry

›Sort by stakes, not by capability. Ask what a mistake would cost — an edit, a patient's safety, or a breach of privacy — before you ask whether AI can do the task.
›AI suggests; the clinician verifies and decides. The tool supports; you own the diagnosis, the plan, and the duty to the patient. AI is decision-support, never the diagnostician of record.
›Patient privacy is a hard line. Identifiable patient data never goes into a consumer tool — convenience never wins that trade.
›Be honest about limits. AI hallucinates evidence, carries bias, is not a cleared device, and cannot examine your patient — verification against primary sources is part of the workflow, not an extra.
