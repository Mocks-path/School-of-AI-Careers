Clinical Information & Evidence with AI — ask sharply, verify always

Three hands-on activities. Build a vague query into a focused PICO question AI can actually help with. Then read AI evidence outputs and flag which ones need primary-source or guideline verification — including a fabricated study and an outdated recommendation. Finally, order the evidence workflow that keeps AI useful and care safe, from question to this patient.

Progress
Q

A sharp question

AI mirrors the question you give it. A vague prompt earns a vague, generic answer; a focused PICO question earns a targeted one you can actually appraise.

V

Verified evidence

AI can fabricate studies and repeat outdated guidance with total confidence. Every reference and recommendation is verified against a primary source before it touches care.

P

This patient

Evidence is a population average. The clinician decides whether it applies to the individual in front of them — comorbidities, values, and context AI cannot see.

1

Build the PICO question — turn a vague query into a focused one

A focused clinical question has four parts: Patient/Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome. Vague in, vague out. For each element, pick the option that makes the question focused and answerable. Choose one per box, then build your PICO question.

🔎 The vague query: "What's the best thing for high blood pressure?" — too broad for AI or any source to answer usefully. Let's sharpen it.
2

Verify or trust? — read the AI evidence output and decide

AI evidence outputs look authoritative even when they are wrong. For each output below, decide whether you can rely on it as-is or must flag it for primary-source or guideline verification. Two are traps: one cites a study that does not exist, one repeats a recommendation that has been superseded. When in doubt, verify.

Order the evidence workflow — from question to this patient

Using evidence safely with AI follows a fixed sequence. AI can help at the start, but the appraisal, the synthesis, and the decision for this patient stay with you. Drag each step into the right order — the appraise & verify step is the one that keeps AI honest, so notice where it sits.

The honest part — three ways AI evidence misleads, and the guard for each

A fluent evidence summary is not a verified one. Three failure modes recur — knowing them, and the guard for each, is what lets you use AI to move faster without moving carelessly.

Fabricated studies Invents evidence

AI produces real-sounding citations — authors, journals, years, even effect sizes — for studies that were never published. A plan built on a phantom trial is built on nothing.

Guard: confirm every reference in a primary source you can open and read. If it is not findable in a real database or guideline, it does not exist.

Outdated recommendations Stale guidance

AI is trained on a snapshot in time and can confidently repeat guidance that has since been revised or reversed. It will not tell you a recommendation has been superseded.

Guard: check any recommendation against the current guideline before you apply it. Recency and the live source decide, not the model's fluent tone.

Population vs. patient Average ≠ individual

Even sound evidence is a population average. AI cannot see your patient's comorbidities, values, or context — applying an average to an individual is a clinical judgment.

Guard: after verifying the evidence, you decide whether it fits this patient. The final step is always yours, informed by evidence but not dictated by it.
💡 Put together, these are one habit: AI helps you ask sharply and orient quickly, but every reference is verified in a primary source, every recommendation is checked for currency, and the fit to this patient is your call. Evidence-informed, clinician-decided.
