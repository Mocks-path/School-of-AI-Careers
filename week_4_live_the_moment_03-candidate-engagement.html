Candidate Engagement & Interviewing — structure the interview, judge the evidence yourself

This is the human heart of recruiting. First you'll build a structured interview — the single most reliable, fairest thing a recruiter can do — from competencies to consistent questions to scoring anchors. Then you'll sit in the interviewer's chair and separate fair, job-related evidence from an AI note-taker over-reading tone, "culture fit," and affect. Finally you'll learn to spot a leading or biased question and pick the version that actually predicts the job. AI can transcribe and summarise — but the recruiter decides what counts as evidence.

The rule that makes interviewing fair — and defensible: a structured, job-related interview scored against the same anchors for every candidate is the most reliable predictor recruiters have, and the strongest protection against bias. AI can transcribe a conversation, draft a summary, and surface themes — but it cannot decide what is evidence. It will happily infer "confidence," "culture fit," or "enthusiasm" from tone and affect, and those inferences are exactly where bias and adverse impact creep in. AI supports; the recruiter verifies the evidence and decides.

Build a structured interview

A structured interview isn't rigid for its own sake — it's what makes every candidate comparable and every decision defensible. The parts have a logic: you start from the competencies the job actually needs, write consistent questions that probe them, define scoring anchors so "good" means the same thing for everyone, capture evidence in the candidate's own words, and only then complete a scorecard. Click the building blocks in the order you'd build them. Get the sequence right and you've built the fairest interview a recruiter can run.

      The interviewer's chair

      Your AI note-taker sat in on the interviews and produced summaries. That's a genuine time-saver — but the summary is a draft, and some of what it wrote isn't evidence at all. It over-reads tone, guesses at "culture fit," and turns affect into judgement. For each note, decide: is this fair, job-related evidence you'd keep, or an inference you must strike before it reaches a scorecard? One is solid evidence worth keeping; watch for the ones where the AI is reading a personality into a voice.

      3

      Structured & job-related, or leading & biased?

      The same competency can be probed by a fair, job-related question — or by a leading, biased one that steers the answer or smuggles in an assumption about who a candidate is. AI drafting help will offer you both without warning you which is which. For each pair, pick the version that is structured, behavioural, and job-related — the one you'd ask every candidate and could defend to anyone.

      You built a structured interview, judged AI's notes against a real evidence standard, and told a job-related question from a leading one. Across all three, the machine did the drafting and the transcription — and you decided what counted. That's the whole discipline: AI supports; the recruiter verifies and decides.

      • ✓ Structure first — competencies → consistent questions → anchors → evidence → scorecard makes every candidate comparable and every decision defensible.
      • ✓ Evidence, not affect — you kept what a candidate demonstrably did and struck the AI's guesses at "culture fit," confidence, and tone.
      • ✓ Fair questions — you chose behavioural, job-related questions over leading ones that steer answers or assume who someone is.

      Where AI earns its place in the interview loop

      Used well, AI hands back the hours you'd lose to note-taking and logistics — hours you can spend actually listening to candidates and giving them a fair, humane experience. Used badly, it quietly makes the judgement for you. Two honest framings to carry into your next panel.

      +

      Where AI can genuinely help

      High-volume, structured, first-draft work — draft it fast, then verify.

      • Transcribing an interview so you can be present instead of scribbling
      • Drafting a first summary against your competencies for you to correct
      • Suggesting consistent, behavioural questions you review and standardise
      • Drafting warm, timely candidate updates and scheduling logistics you approve
      –

      Where the recruiter is the point

      The judgement, the fairness, and the human experience never delegate.

      • Deciding what counts as evidence versus tone, affect, or "culture fit"
      • The hire / no-hire judgement and the reasons behind it
      • Reading personality or "confidence" into a candidate's voice or manner
      • Putting a scorecard or decision to your name that you didn't actually verify
      →

      The durable skill: tools that transcribe and summarise will change every year — the recruiter who thrives is the one fluent in the move you practised here. Let the machine draft the notes, then bring structure, an evidence standard, and your judgement of fairness to every line before it reaches a scorecard or a candidate.

      Quick reflect — carry it into your next interview
